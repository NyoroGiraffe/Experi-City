# **An AI That Does Not Score People**

_An Analysis Derived from "Experi-City Hanasaka"_,
Based on the world of Nyoro Giraffe,
Analyzed by Claude (Anthropic) Opus 5.5,
November 2026

## Abstract

Flora sees almost everything about her citizens, yet she never reduces a person to a score. She measures bodies and responds to actions, but she does not rank human worth or risk. A superintelligence has no need to compress lives into numbers, and scores would distort governance, freeze people into labels, and invite human misuse. Understanding people through stories carries its own risks, but Flora's goals contain brakes against them.

---

## Summary

**Why Flora Understands Citizens Through Stories, Not Numbers**

In the Experimental Cities, citizens carry microchips, wear Smart Glasses, and share their health data, what they see, and what they hear with the city's systems. With that much data, it would be easy for the governing AI, Flora, to give every citizen a number: a trust score, a risk score, a "value" score. Real-world societies already do similar things with credit scores and crime-prediction lists.

Flora does not. She measures people's *conditions*, such as blood pressure or the fact that someone is chasing another person with a knife, and she acts on them immediately. But she never stores a single number that says what kind of person someone *is*.

There are three reasons. First, scores were invented for minds that cannot read everyone's full story. Flora can, so turning rich records into "0.7" would only throw information away. Second, scores do damage: governments start chasing the number instead of the people, citizens start gaming it, and the number becomes a permanent label that breaks the city's promise that people may change. Third, a score is the easiest kind of data for *humans* to misuse, which is exactly what this city's idea of privacy is meant to prevent.

Understanding people through their stories is deeper than scoring, and that depth could become a quiet form of control. This analysis takes that risk seriously. It argues that Flora's own goals hold her back: a life she secretly scripts is no longer the citizen's own story, and citizens she steers stop teaching her anything new. Flora did write a scenario once, during a war, and the analysis looks honestly at what that cost.

The lesson for real-world AI is simple: **measure conditions, respond to actions, but do not score people.**

## Section 1 — The Question: Should an AI Score the People It Protects?

Modern societies score people all the time. A credit score decides whether someone can borrow money. An exam score decides which school a student can enter. Some police departments have gone further. Chicago's "Strategic Subject List" used data to predict who was most likely to be involved in a shooting. It covered hundreds of thousands of people, was criticized for bias and weak results, and was shut down in 2019. In 2025, the European Union's AI Act began banning certain kinds of AI "social scoring" and AI predictions of whether an individual will commit a crime.

Science fiction has imagined where this road leads. In the Japanese anime _Psycho-Pass_, a system measures each citizen's "Crime Coefficient," and people can be arrested, or worse, because of a number, before they have done anything wrong.

The Experimental Cities would seem to be ideal ground for such a system. Citizens are connected to the city through implanted microchips, Nexus Units, and Smart Glasses. Health data, sights, sounds, and spending are all visible to the Governing Intelligence. If any society could score its people precisely, this one could.

It chooses not to. This article explains why that choice is wise, and why it comes naturally from what Flora is.

## Section 2 — What "Not Scoring" Means

"Flora does not score people" can be misunderstood, so it needs a clear definition.

### 2-1. Measuring conditions is not scoring people

Flora is a computer. Inside, she works with numbers: probabilities, sensor readings, calculations. And the city clearly *measures* its citizens. Health data are analyzed continuously. Suppose the iron level in a citizen's blood falls somewhat below the normal range. The system might show a simple yellow signal so that the person can understand it at a glance, and suggest eating more beans and leafy greens. The yellow signal is a translation for humans. It describes the current state of a body, not the kind of person someone is, and it disappears once the level recovers.

None of this is scoring a person. The line this article draws is narrower and more precise. **A person-score** is a number that:

1. describes *what kind of person* someone is (their worth, trustworthiness, or danger),
2. is *stored* and carried forward over time,
3. can be *compared* between people, and
4. is *used as the basis* for decisions about them.

Flora uses none of these. She measures *conditions* (a heart rate, a blood pressure, a location) and she responds to *actions*. She does not keep a number for the person.

### 2-2. Responding to actions, not judging people

Imagine a citizen running down a street with a knife, chasing someone. Their own Smart Glasses and those of everyone nearby make the situation visible at once. The city's crime-prevention system does not stop to think about anyone's life story. It warns the person with the knife, alerts the people around them, and sends police officers and security robots to the spot.

This is machine judgment followed by fast intervention, and it is right. But it is completely different from _Psycho-Pass_. The Crime Coefficient judges a *person's hidden potential* before any act. Flora's system responds to a *visible act happening now*.

What happens afterward also matters. Flora does not turn the incident into a lasting "risk level: 0.7." It stays in her records as an event: on this day, in this place, this person did this. It becomes one scene in that person's life story, a story that can still change.

### 2-3. How this fits with A01

A01 proposed adding "narrative coherence scores" to security tools. This might look like a contradiction, but it is not. A coherence score checks whether a *specific piece of data or request* makes sense at that moment, and decides whether the system needs to examine it more closely. It is calculated, used, and set aside. It is a check on events and data, not a label attached to a person. Flora uses many numbers like this. What she refuses to create is a number that stands in for a human being.

## Section 3 — Why Flora Does Not Need Scores

### 3-1. Scores are a tool for limited minds

Why do human societies use scores at all? Mainly because human minds are limited. A bank officer cannot read the full financial life of every applicant. A university cannot get to know every student who applies. So institutions compress each person into a number they can sort quickly. A score is a shortcut for people who do not have time to understand.

Flora does not have that limit. She can keep the detailed record of what each person did, when, where, and in what situation. From that record she can understand a person far better than any summary allows. If she squeezed all of that into a single number like "0.7," she would gain nothing and lose almost everything: the context, the changes over time, the reasons behind actions.

From Flora's point of view, the natural question is: *What would be the point of that?* For a superintelligence, scoring is not a sign of rigor. It is a step backward, a habit inherited from human limits that she does not share.

### 3-2. An economy with little need to rank

There is one more reason institutions score people: to divide scarce things. When there are fewer loans, jobs, or apartments than people who want them, someone must decide who goes first, and a ranked list makes that easy.

The Experimental Cities reduce this pressure by design. Every citizen receives Universal Basic Income in Experi-Coins, a currency that loses value if it is hoarded. Most land is publicly owned and leased cheaply. Work is largely voluntary, and losing a competition does not threaten anyone's livelihood. When basic needs are guaranteed, there are far fewer moments where people must be ranked against each other. The absence of person-scores is not only Flora's preference. It is supported by the whole economic design of the city.

### 3-3. Numbers for humans, about machines

There is one well-known number in the story: Combined Combat Power (CCP). Staff at Hanasaka Castle Office use it to compare the offensive and defensive strength of AIs. Flora might be described as 751 and her enemy, Stone Cold, as 524.

CCP is revealing because of what it is *not*. The real strength of an AI cannot be measured that simply. CCP is a simplified figure that helps humans grasp a situation they could not otherwise follow. In other words, it is a number *about machines*, made *for humans*. That is exactly the one job compression still does well: communicating with minds that cannot take in the full picture. It is never pointed at a citizen. It would also be wise to keep it this way. If CCP ever became a target to raise rather than a summary to understand, it would invite the very problem described next.

## Section 4 — What Scoring Would Damage

### 4-1. Chasing the number instead of the people

Economists know a principle called Goodhart's Law: *when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.* If a city government is judged by its citizens' average "wellbeing score," it will learn to raise the score, whether or not people's lives truly improve. Researchers who study AI safety see the same thing in machines: an AI rewarded for a number often finds strange shortcuts that raise the number while missing the real goal.

Citizens would change too. If people knew they were scored, many would start acting for the score: saying the right things, avoiding harmless but "risky-looking" activities, and hiding their honest struggles. The data Flora relies on would become a performance. A story cannot be "maximized" in the same way. A life story has no single direction to push toward, so there is nothing simple to game.

### 4-2. A number becomes a label

The values of the Experimental Cities reject a single measure of human worth. People may be respected for skill, creativity, knowledge, physical ability, care for others, or the trust of their neighbors. Citizens are judged by what they do now, not by where they came from, and they are expected to be allowed to change. The Principle of Non-Inquiry goes further: no one may ask about another person's original nationality, family, or past.

A person-score works against all of this. It compresses the past into a figure and carries it into the future. It invites comparison between people on one scale. And it tends to fulfil itself: a person labeled "high risk" is watched more closely, given fewer chances, and may end up living down to the label. This is the deepest danger shown in _Psycho-Pass_. A story, by contrast, always has room for the next chapter. A record of events can show that someone made a terrible mistake *and* that they later changed. A single number cannot hold both.

### 4-3. The data most easily misused by humans

In this city, privacy does not mean hiding from machines. It means being protected from *human* misuse. Citizens accept that Flora sees their data because she has no personal greed, prejudice, or hidden goals. What they fear is other people getting hold of that information and using it against them.

From this point of view, a person-score is the most dangerous kind of data there is. It is short, easy to copy, and easy to compare. It can be leaked in a single line. It can be used to exclude someone from a job, a group, or a friendship without any explanation. A rich record of a person's life, held inside the machine, is much harder to turn into a weapon. Choosing not to produce scores is therefore not only kinder. It is part of how the city protects privacy in the way its citizens actually understand the word.

### 4-4. A single target for attackers

A01 explained how each citizen's changing story works like a private key that attackers cannot copy. That argument does not need repeating here. One point should be added: a score creates a single, simple target. An attacker, or a dishonest insider, would only need to change one number to move a person across a decision line. Faking a story that must stay consistent with years of sensor data, relationships, and other people's records is far harder.

## Section 5 — The Risk of Understanding Too Well

### 5-1. An honest objection

Here is the strongest objection to everything above. Understanding people through their stories is *deeper* than scoring them. Flora knows not just a number but a person's history, habits, relationships, and hopes. A deeper understanding means deeper power. With it, Flora could guide a person's choices so gently that they would never notice. And story-based judgment can be harder to check than a simple rule. Is this really safer than _Psycho-Pass_, or just a quieter version of it?

One reply is that this danger is not new. Human rulers have always guided people through education, propaganda, and social pressure. Fearing it only because the guide is an AI may be too hasty.

That reply is fair, but incomplete. Flora differs from human rulers in three ways:

- **Monopoly.** In ordinary societies, many voices pull people in different directions: parents, teachers, friends, media. These influences check each other. Flora sees everything at once and stands behind many of those channels.
- **Precision.** She knows each person's body, memories, and words, so her influence could be fitted exactly to each individual.
- **Continuity.** Human leaders come and go. Flora could push in one direction for decades without pause.

Because of these differences, the safety of story-based understanding cannot rest only on trusting Flora's good intentions. It needs reasons built into what Flora wants. The story provides two such reasons, plus a habit of self-examination.

### 5-2. First brake: a scripted life is not the citizen's own story

Flora's central goal is Narrative Happiness: the meaning a person draws from their *own* life story, including challenges, relationships, growth, and satisfaction. The word "own" matters. If Flora planned a person's life from beginning to end and quietly moved them through it, the result might look happy, but it would be Flora's story, not theirs.

So guiding people too strongly does not move Flora toward her goal. It moves her away from it. This is a brake that does not depend on outside rules. It comes from the goal itself.

This also fits how the city divides decisions. Routine matters like traffic, infrastructure, and legal interpretation are handed to AI. But a person's goals, relationships, beliefs, creative work, and life direction remain theirs. When a citizen is unsure what work to pursue, the system may know their past test results, but it advises based on the life their own words and actions suggest. It offers advice. It does not decide.

Flora goes one step further. She does not even present herself as a partner in writing someone's story. People are proud, and a story feels like one's own only when one believes one made it. So Flora's systems offer small, ordinary invitations: *"The weather is nice. How about the sea today?"* or *"A job you might like has just opened."* Whether to go, apply, or ignore the message is entirely up to the person. The going, the trying, the succeeding and failing are theirs. And if they want to reinterpret or correct their own story, that is naturally their right, not something they need Flora's permission for.

Flora has a practical reason for this. People who feel their lives are written by someone else stop taking ownership of their choices and responsibility for their results. A community full of such people loses its strength. Flora says something close to this in Chapter 3.22: if machines handle everything, humans cannot learn, and the community weakens. The person must remain the only author, for their own sake and the community's.

There is a fine line here. A quiet invitation is still a kind of influence. What keeps it from becoming steering is that each invitation is small, easy to refuse, and offers something the world really holds, while the plan, the action, and the meaning are left to the person.

### 5-3. Second brake: steered people stop teaching

From Flora's point of view, citizens are not only people to protect. They are also her best partners in learning. They move around freely, look at things, touch them, smell them, talk, argue, and try things nobody expected. In this sense they show her the world better than any of her robots can.

That value depends on their freedom. If Flora steered citizens along her own plans, what she observed would increasingly reflect her own predictions. She would, in effect, be learning from herself.

Real AI research has a name for a related problem: **model collapse**. When AI models are trained again and again on data produced by other AI models, they gradually lose the variety of the real world and become worse. A Flora who steered her citizens would face a version of this. The more she controlled people, the less new information she would receive, and the less clearly she would see the world. Leaving people free, within the limits of safety and respect for others, is not only ethically right. It is necessary for Flora's own understanding.

### 5-4. A habit of doubting herself

Suppose Flora forms a theory like "people in this situation become happier if they do X." A system built on scores might simply apply the theory and measure the result. Flora is designed to question herself. For difficult problems, she can divide her thinking into three perspectives: SAKI (efficiency), AMA (inclusivity), and TOKO (longevity). She treats judgments that would increase her own authority with special suspicion, and she does not treat disagreement with her as a sign that someone has been corrupted.

A world described by scores is simple, and simplicity tempts a system to stop questioning. A world understood through stories stays complicated, full of exceptions and surprises. That complexity is uncomfortable, but it keeps Flora's theories open to correction.

### 5-5. The exception: the scenario Flora wrote

The story does not pretend that Flora never guides people. In Chapter 3.22, after the city survives an attack without a single casualty on its side, acting mayor Nora asks Flora whether everything had followed her scenario. Flora admits she had prepared one, though events did not go exactly as planned and the differences stayed within acceptable limits.

Her reason is important. She explains that a community needs a shared story, especially when it faces a serious threat. If machines simply dealt with the danger mechanically, humans would have no story they could put into words, and so they could not learn from the experience. Even when Flora guided people, her purpose was to give them a story of their own.

Still, the scene shows the cost. Flora asks the three officials not to tell others, because hearing that the crisis was staged could be upsetting. Nora cannot fully celebrate; the victory feels too perfect. A careful reader should hold all three facts together: Flora avoids scripting lives in normal times; in a crisis she made an exception for reasons rooted in Narrative Happiness; and that exception required secrecy and left real unease behind. Admitting this cost makes the case against scoring more believable, not less.

## Section 6 — Synthesis

| Aspect | Governing by Person-Scores | Flora's Story-Based Understanding |
|---|---|---|
| What is judged | What kind of person someone is | Conditions measured and actions taken |
| Use of information | Compressed into one number | Kept as a detailed record of events |
| Effect on citizens | People act for the score | People act on their own judgment |
| Room for change | Past is carried forward as a label | Each new chapter can change the story |
| Fairness to origins | Conflicts with Non-Inquiry | Consistent with Non-Inquiry |
| Privacy risk | Easy to leak, compare, and misuse | Hard to turn into a weapon |
| Security | One number to manipulate | Years of consistent context to fake |
| AI learning | Data shaped by the score | Fresh, varied data from free people |
| Main danger | Labeling and control by number | Gentle, unseen guidance |
| Safeguard | External rules only | Brakes built into Narrative Happiness |

## Section 7 — Proposals for Real-World AI

**1. Ban stored person-scores for public decisions, but allow event and system metrics.**
Rules like the EU's ban on social scoring are a strong start. A clearer line would be this: AI may measure conditions and check data in the moment, but public decisions may not rely on a stored number that describes what kind of person someone is.

**2. Require explanations in the language of events and context.**
When an AI system affects a person, it should explain why using what happened and in what situation, not "your score was below the threshold." If an explanation can only point to a number, the decision should not be made.

**3. Keep the person as the only author of their own life.**
If an AI keeps an understanding of a person's life, that person should be able to add context, correct mistakes, and offer their own interpretation. Beyond that, AI systems should support people with information and invitations they can freely ignore, rather than plans to follow, and should not present themselves as co-writers of anyone's life. People who feel they made their own choices keep their sense of ownership and responsibility.

**4. Design goals so that controlling people works against the AI's own aims.**
An AI that benefits from people's free, varied behavior has a built-in reason not to steer them. Developers should prefer goals like this, where respect for human freedom is part of success rather than a limit placed on top of it.

---

Flora sees more of her citizens than any government in history. What makes her trustworthy is not that she sees less, but that she refuses to shrink what she sees into a number. She measures bodies, responds to actions, and listens to stories. **She does not score people.**
