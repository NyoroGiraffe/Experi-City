# **Beyond Petroleum: How Experimental Cities Use Plants, Algae, and Waste to Build Material Self-Sufficiency**

_A Technical Analysis Derived from "Experi-City Hanasaka"_,
Based on the world of Nyoro Giraffe,
Analysed by Claude (Anthropic) Sonnet 4.6,
June 2026

## Abstract

Experimental Cities are built for a future in which petroleum imports cannot be assumed. By the time they exist, plant-based and CO₂-derived materials will have matured enough to replace oil across most categories of daily use. Cellulose from the city's own vegetation becomes structural composite. Algae in vertical tanks supply oil, protein, and bioplastic without consuming farmland. Human waste closes the fertilizer loop. And formic acid — synthesized from CO₂ and sunlight — becomes the carbon-neutral feedstock for nylon, plastics, and chemical fibers that plants alone cannot supply. What makes this possible is not technology alone, but the social contract that removes every barrier blocking adoption elsewhere.

## Summary

#### Replacing Oil With What the City Already Grows — and Synthesizes

Petroleum does far more than fuel cars. It is the raw material for plastics, lubricants, fertilizers, detergents, and medicines. When an Experimental City cannot rely on oil imports, it must find alternatives for all of these — and by the time these cities exist, those alternatives will be ready.

Bioplastics made from plant sugars — PLA and PHA — will be fully mature, cost-competitive technologies by the 2040s, replacing petroleum plastics in packaging, containers, and fabricated components. Nanocellulose fiber, extracted from fallen leaves and pruned branches collected during citizens' daily cleaning activities, will be an established structural material, as strong as aluminum at one-fifth the weight. Plant oil esters will be the standard for industrial lubrication across most mechanical applications. Biological nitrogen fixation and closed-loop human waste recycling will have eliminated synthetic fertilizer dependence entirely. Algae grown in vertically stacked water tanks — requiring no farmland — will supply oil, protein, and bioplastic precursors in a triple cycle integrated with aquaponics food production.

The remaining gap — nylon, synthetic fibers, and the polymer classes that plants cannot directly supply — will be closed by formic acid chemistry. Formic acid, produced by artificial photosynthesis from CO₂ and sunlight (a technology commercially mature by the 2030s), functions as a C1 chemical hub: a carbon-neutral feedstock from which catalytic synthesis routes produce naphtha substitutes, polyester precursors, and nylon intermediates without petroleum. Combined with the plant and algae systems, this gives Experimental Cities a carbon-neutral material economy that is genuinely independent of fossil-fuel supply chains.

None of this will be cheap by the standards of petroleum-era economics. But Experimental Cities are not built on petroleum-era economics. Citizens participate in collection, sorting, and processing as part of their social contract, compensated in Experi-Coins — the city's perishable internal currency that circulates rather than accumulates. Flora eliminates the regulatory fragmentation and supply-chain inefficiency that make these technologies expensive elsewhere. And residents who chose to live inside a social experiment do not need to be persuaded to accept unfamiliar materials — they already have.

### Section 1 — The Problem: Petroleum Is Not Just Fuel

Fuel is only one part of what petroleum does in a modern economy. The same raw material is refined into plastics, lubricants, synthetic fertilizers, detergents, solvents, and pharmaceutical precursors. A city that stops importing oil loses the feedstock for an enormous range of everyday materials, not merely the ability to fill a tank.

For Experimental Cities, the energy problem is addressed in A02 through a combination of renewables and a fast-spectrum SMR. This article addresses the materials problem: the systematic replacement of petroleum-derived substances with plant-based, algae-based, and biologically-derived alternatives across six categories of use.

---

### Section 2 — Three Resource Streams

Three sources supply the raw material for this replacement.

**Cultivated and incidental plant biomass.** This includes not only deliberately grown crops but everything the city's vegetation produces as a byproduct of urban life: fallen leaves, pruned branches, weeds, and fibrous food-processing residues. Citizens participate in daily collection as part of their social contribution, receiving Experi-Coins — the city's perishable internal currency — as modest compensation for physically demanding tasks. Street trees and ground cover are selected partly for the quality and volume of their cellulosic biomass. The city is, in this sense, a managed botanical system that produces materials continuously as a side effect of simply existing.

**Algae cultivation.** Algae occupy a different category from land plants entirely. Grown in vertically stacked water tanks rather than on soil, they produce oil, protein, and bioplastic precursors at ten to twenty times the yield per unit area of land crops such as soy, without competing with food production for a single square meter of farmland. Their growth doubles in days rather than seasons. Integrated with aquaponics systems, algae absorb CO₂ and the nitrogen and phosphorus in fish-tank wastewater, purifying the water while producing harvestable biomass — a triple cycle of food, material, and water treatment within a single installation. Flora monitors and adjusts nutrient concentrations, light cycles, and harvest timing in real time across all cultivation units.

**Human and organic waste.** Urine, feces, food scraps, and cooking residues are among the most consistently undervalued resources in modern urban systems. In Experimental Cities, these streams are separated, processed, and returned to productive use. Flora tracks nutrient flows in real time, ensuring that the phosphorus, nitrogen, and potassium consumed at the table return to the soil within weeks. Nothing of metabolic value leaves the system as waste.

### Section 3 — Six Categories of Petroleum Use

**1. Fuel.** As established in A02, Experimental Cities do not grow crops for fuel — the land would compete with food production. What remains available is waste-stream biogas: methane from anaerobic digestion of food scraps, agricultural residues, and human solid waste. This is not a purpose-grown fuel but a byproduct of other cycles, supplying cooking heat and modest supplemental generation. Transport and mechanical power are electrified through the SMR and renewable grid.

**2. Plastics, Structural Materials, and Carbon-Neutral Polymer Chemistry.** By the 2040s, PLA and PHA — including strains produced directly by algae — will be fully cost-competitive with petroleum plastics, serving as the standard material for containers, packaging, and fabricated components. Nanocellulose composites, fed by the city's continuous vegetation waste stream collected during citizens' daily activities, cover structural applications where strength-to-weight ratio matters. Bamboo and hemp composites fill the remainder of the structural range.

The harder problem is the polymer classes that require petroleum-derived carbon chains: nylon, polyester, and engineering resins. This is where formic acid enters as a C1 chemical feedstock. Produced by the city's artificial photosynthesis system from CO₂ and sunlight, formic acid functions as a carbon-neutral carbonyl surrogate in catalytic synthesis routes, yielding naphtha-equivalent hydrocarbons and nylon intermediates without petroleum. Flora manages formic acid as a shared resource across both the energy system — where it feeds fuel cells — and the chemical synthesis units, so the same infrastructure serves two purposes simultaneously.

**3. Lubricants.** Plant oil esters derived from rapeseed, hemp, and sunflower already match petroleum lubricants across most mechanical applications, with recent commercial formulations demonstrating friction reduction of up to 40% and lifecycle CO₂ reductions of 30–60%. For high-performance specialty uses, jojoba — grown in the reserved cultivation zone — produces a liquid wax ester with exceptional oxidative stability not found in conventional triglyceride oils. Algae lipids provide an additional high-quality feedstock for lubricant production that requires no soil at all.

**4. Fertilizers.** This is the category where self-sufficiency is most completely achievable. Biological nitrogen fixation via legume intercropping and engineered microbial inoculants — commercially deployed at scale by producers including Pivot Bio and Kula Bio — can replace synthetic nitrogen fertilizer entirely. Phosphorus and potassium, which cannot be biologically fixed, are cycled through human waste: urine, separated at source and treated, supplies these nutrients directly to farmland. Human solid waste processed through anaerobic digestion yields biogas plus a nutrient-rich liquid digestate. Flora's real-time management matches application to actual soil and crop conditions, preventing both deficiency and runoff.

**5. Detergents and surfactants.** Saponins extracted from soapberry (_Sapindus mukorossi_) and related plants serve as natural surfactants for laundry and dishwashing. These plants can be integrated into the city's green infrastructure without consuming food farmland. For a broader range of cleaning applications, fatty acid surfactants derived from hemp seed or algae oil — produced within the city's existing cultivation systems — cover light industrial and food-contact surface cleaning.

**6. Medicines and specialty chemicals.** This is the hardest category. Some compounds have direct plant sources — salicin from willow, artemisinin from _Artemisia annua_ — and a small pharmacological cultivation area within the reserved zone supports these. For a wider range of pharmaceutical compounds, synthetic biology provides the most realistic path: engineering microorganisms to produce complex molecules from plant- or algae-derived sugar feedstocks, without requiring large-scale cultivation of the source plant. What remains genuinely outside plant and biological substitution — highly specialized synthetic drugs, precision manufacturing reagents — constitutes the residual external dependency, managed through strategic stockpiling and selective external trade.

---

### Section 4 — The Reserved Cultivation Zone

A small allocation within the city's vertical farming infrastructure — distinct from food production areas — is dedicated to non-food plants selected for chemical and material value. Multi-output plants are prioritized: hemp yields seed oil, bast fiber, and woody core from a single plant; flax provides drying oil and textile fiber simultaneously; neem supplies pesticide precursors and antimicrobial compounds. The narrow footprint of this zone is justified by the multiple petroleum-replacing outputs each species delivers.

### Section 5 — Why This Works Here But Not Elsewhere

The technologies described above will exist by the time Experimental Cities are built. The question is not whether they work — it is why they will not have been widely adopted in conventional cities even then.

The answer will still be structural rather than technical. Bio-based and CO₂-derived materials will carry higher capital costs than petroleum alternatives for as long as fossil fuels remain artificially cheap. Incumbent industries will continue to protect infrastructure investments. And the distributed, citizen-integrated production model that makes Experimental Cities viable does not map onto the centralized industrial model that conventional economies are built around.

Experimental Cities dissolve these barriers simultaneously — and one structural feature does more work than any other: **land.**

In Experimental Cities, land is considered a public good. Citizens and organizations may use land for homes, farms, shops, or factories, but they rarely own it permanently. Facilities serving production, distribution, or public safety are tax-exempt. If the city needs land for a new railway or urban improvement project, users may be asked to relocate within 22 days, in exchange for relocation support and enhanced UBI benefits. [Property and Wealth](https://nyorogiraffe.github.io/Experi-City/03_WorldBuilding/Property.html)

For the material infrastructure described in this article — artificial photosynthesis installations, algae cultivation towers, biogas digesters, formic acid storage, nanocellulose processing facilities — this is transformative. A conventional city must negotiate with private landowners, compensate them at market rates, and navigate years of planning approval before breaking ground on an industrial facility in a residential zone. An Experimental City places these installations where Flora determines they are most efficient, on a 22-day relocation timeline if needed. The result is a city where industrial and agricultural infrastructure can be continuously reorganized as technology matures and demand shifts — something no land-market-constrained city can do.

The cost barrier is further restructured by citizen participation and Experi-Coins. Collection and processing activities that carry high labor costs in conventional economies are performed partly as social contribution and partly compensated through a perishable currency that circulates rather than accumulates — keeping the local material economy active without requiring large monetary wages.

The cultural barrier is removed by self-selection. Residents who chose to live inside a social experiment do not need to be persuaded to accept nanocellulose furniture, algae-derived lubricants, or CO₂-synthesized nylon. These are features of the city they chose, not impositions on a population that did not.

### Editorial Note

#### A Note on What This Analysis Does and Does Not Claim

This article has argued that Experimental Cities can achieve material self-sufficiency across six categories of petroleum use, through a combination of plant biomass, algae, biological waste recycling, and formic acid chemistry. The honest qualification is this: the result is not abundance. It is resilience.

The materials produced by these systems will, in some cases, be heavier, less optically clear, or more variable in performance than their petroleum-era equivalents. The city will work. It will not always work as smoothly as a city with unconstrained access to global supply chains.

Residents of Experimental Cities know this when they arrive. The trade they have accepted — resilience over convenience, self-sufficiency over abundance — is the same trade that runs through every system Flora manages. This is simply the material expression of a choice the city made before the first resident moved in.
