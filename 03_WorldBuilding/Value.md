# Values and Social Participation in Experimental Cities

## Abstract

In Experimental Cities, UBI allows citizens to work, create, and contribute without making survival their main concern. Society values personal growth, cooperation, creative expression, and Narrative Happiness rather than wealth or inherited status. Competition remains, but it is mainly voluntary. Citizens build their identities through present actions and relationships, while new forms of leadership can emerge from trust, ability, and service to the community.

---

## 1. Work and Social Contribution

### Human Value Beyond Economic Survival

In Experimental Cities, Universal Basic Income (UBI) allows every citizen to meet basic needs without depending on employment. As a result, people do not have to treat work simply as a means of survival. They can choose activities based on their abilities, interests, relationships, and desire to contribute to society.

A common criticism of UBI is that people will stop working if they can live without a job, causing society to lose the labor it needs. Experimental Cities challenge this assumption. Although some citizens may choose not to work, many find purpose in developing skills, completing meaningful tasks, helping others, and taking part in their communities.

There is no social stigma attached to not working. At the same time, voluntary contribution is respected, and labor is regarded as a meaningful way to participate in society. UBI changes the reasons people work; it does not make work meaningless.

### The Changing Nature of Labor

AI and robots perform much of the work that does not require distinctively human judgment or interaction.

- **AI and robots:** AI handles digital processing, high-speed logical operations, and most management tasks. Robots perform repetitive labor and tasks requiring extreme strength.
    
- **AI as the boss:** Decisions, approvals, and other management functions are mostly handled by AI. The farther a position is from the actual work site, the more likely it is to be held by an AI rather than a human.
    
- **Humans in the field:** Human work focuses more on tasks requiring flexible responses, practical judgment, empathy, and the ability to deal with complex, changing circumstances.
    
- **Community contribution:** Citizens are encouraged to help with agriculture, forestry, fisheries, and infrastructure maintenance. They may take part in these activities during their free time, even when the work offers little or no direct pay. Such participation can be a natural way to give back to the community that supports them through UBI.

In this society, holding a management position or controlling decisions does not automatically make someone more valuable than a person doing practical work. Human contribution is not measured only by income, rank, or authority.

### Essential Workers and Crisis Priority

Essential workers maintain social infrastructure and public safety and protect the lives and health of citizens. Their work may require specialized skills, practical judgment, and the ability to respond to difficult or unexpected situations.

As AI takes over many management and administrative tasks, skilled human workers who can maintain essential services and respond to real-world needs become especially important. Those with valuable expertise and proven abilities are likely to receive good compensation, even though UBI already guarantees their basic needs. Such compensation can recognize their skills, responsibilities, and the value of investing in their training and development.

Essential workers also have a special position in extreme crises. If a disaster makes it impossible to provide assistance to everyone, those maintaining essential services may receive priority assistance because the community depends on their continued work. Other citizens must accept that, in such circumstances, they may be left without assistance.

This priority reflects the importance of maintaining the functions on which the whole community depends. It does not mean that citizens who do not perform essential work are less worthy as human beings.

## 2. Creative Activity and Intellectual Property

### Creative Activity and Narrative Happiness

Creative activities, including writing, music, art, invention, and community projects, are valued for the meaning, connection, and Narrative Happiness they can create.

Even when AI can produce technically superior works, human creativity remains important because it expresses people's choices, feelings, experiences, and ways of seeing the world. Creative activity allows people to develop their abilities, share ideas, and contribute to the culture of their community.

Since UBI supports basic living needs, people can pursue creative work without depending entirely on its commercial success. This gives them greater freedom to explore ideas, collaborate with others, and create works that may be meaningful even when they do not attract a large audience.

### Respect for Creators Without Exclusive Ownership

Experimental Cities respect the people who originate creative works and inventions. Giving credit to creators matters, and presenting another person's work as one's own is not acceptable. However, being the creator of a work does not give a person an unlimited right to exclude others from accessing or using it.

Except for ordinary personal-use goods and other specifically recognized cases, goods and knowledge needed by society are not considered things that any particular person should be able to monopolize. Accordingly, Experimental Cities do not recognize exclusive intellectual property rights that allow creators or investors to permanently control access to socially needed works, knowledge, or resources.

This does not mean that copying or modifying works in violation of established rules is freely permitted. A creator or other affected party may demand that such misconduct stop. These claims help deter dishonest conduct and protect the integrity of creative activity without granting an unlimited monopoly over the work itself.

### Compensation and Investment

Creators may receive compensation through commissions, awards, grants, or payments for the use of their works. Investors may also receive agreed benefits in return for their support, including special access or usage rights. In arrangements such as Civic Bonds, the original contribution is not repaid in money; instead, its recognition takes the form of defined non-monetary benefits. Such arrangements can reward creativity and investment without creating permanent, interest-bearing claims or unrestricted monopolies over socially needed resources.

The aim is to recognize creative effort, encourage investment, deter misconduct, and allow culture and knowledge to remain broadly accessible. UBI supports this approach by allowing people to create without depending on exclusive ownership for their basic livelihood.

## 3. Competition, Growth, and Cooperation

### Competition After Survival Is Guaranteed

When basic needs are secured through UBI, competition for survival becomes less central to social life. However, this raises an important question: without the threat of poverty or failure, will people lose their motivation to work hard? Will those who make the greatest efforts go unrewarded, and will society lose the benefits of competition and natural selection?

Experimental Cities do not seek to eliminate competition or differences in achievement. They seek to separate the rewards of effort and ability from the threat of losing one's basic livelihood. People can still pursue difficult goals, compete to demonstrate their skills, and earn recognition or greater rewards. The difference is that losing does not mean losing the right to live with dignity.

The city's challenge is to encourage effort, growth, and excellence without making human survival depend on defeating others.

### Growth, Recognition, and Cooperation

People may value different things: mastery of a skill, creative achievement, physical ability, knowledge, social contribution, or the trust of others. There is no single measure that fully captures a person's value.

Recognition can motivate people to improve, but it need not take the form of wealth or formal authority. A person may be respected for solving a difficult problem, supporting a friend, teaching others, or helping a community overcome a challenge.

Cooperation is equally important. Many achievements depend on shared knowledge, mutual assistance, and the contributions of people with different abilities. Competition and cooperation can coexist when people pursue individual goals while also recognizing their dependence on others.

The goal is not to eliminate ambition or differences in achievement. It is to prevent those differences from automatically becoming control over other people's lives or access to the resources they need.

## 4. Citizenship, Identity, and New Elites

### Citizenship and Personal Identity

#### Why Discard Nationality and Family Name?

In Experimental Cities, citizens leave behind their original nationality and family name and choose a new family name. This does not erase their memories or deny their past. It prevents inherited labels, family reputations, and old political divisions from determining how people are treated.

Citizens are expected to build their identities through their present actions, abilities, relationships, and contributions. Their past may remain part of their personal story, but it should not decide their place in the community.

#### Why "Do Not Ask"?

The Principle of Non-Inquiry prohibits asking about other people's original nationality, family background, or personal history. Curiosity does not automatically give someone the right to know.

Even seemingly harmless questions can revive prejudice and cause people to be judged as representatives of a country, family, or social group rather than as individuals. Non-Inquiry protects privacy and allows people to build new relationships without having to defend their past.

#### Does Silence Still Have Value When People Choose to Share?

Yes. Non-Inquiry does not prohibit voluntary sharing. Citizens may tell trusted friends about their past when they wish to deepen a relationship.

The principle protects their right to choose whether, when, and with whom to share. When disclosure is not demanded, sharing becomes an act of trust rather than a condition for acceptance. Silence and intimacy can therefore coexist.

#### How Do People Stay United After Learning Each Other's Secrets?

Learning someone's past does not have to define their relationship. Citizens can understand one another more deeply without reducing anyone to their origins or past mistakes.

Their unity comes not from shared ancestry or identical histories, but from their shared commitment to live under the city's principles and build a future together. Past actions may matter, but people must also be allowed to change.

#### Why Does the City Reject the "Powerful"?

Experimental Cities place strict conditions on bringing substantial outside wealth and inherited status into the community. This may discourage even talented and generous people. Does Flora prefer the weak and poor to the strong and wealthy?

The issue is not whether rich people are good or bad. Wealth, reputation, and connections can give newcomers influence that ordinary citizens cannot match, allowing old hierarchies to reappear. This could undermine UBI and the city's efforts to prevent wealth from becoming concentrated.

Flora therefore treats imported wealth and status as systemic risks, not proof of personal wrongdoing. The city accepts that these restrictions may cost it some valuable contributions, because preventing inherited power from being restored is more important than attracting everyone who might be useful.

The goal is not to eliminate differences in ability or achievement, but to ensure that people build their standing within the community rather than bring an established hierarchy with them.

This principle also shapes the city's view of inheritance. People may preserve and pass on meaningful belongings, creative works, knowledge, and cultural traditions, but monetary wealth and personal economic privileges are not inherited. Since UBI provides basic living security, family members do not need to inherit wealth to secure their future. Experimental Cities seek to preserve cultural continuity without allowing family background to determine access to economic power.

### The New Elite: Leadership Without Inherited Power

Differences in ability, experience, achievement, and social influence still emerge. Some people may become respected leaders because they have exceptional skills, earn the trust of others, or consistently serve their communities.

These people may form a new kind of elite, but their standing is not supposed to depend on inherited wealth or permanent control over essential resources. Their influence is grounded more in what they can do and the trust they have earned than in what they own.

This does not mean that influence will be distributed equally or that social hierarchies will disappear. Expertise and reputation can create real differences in status. The important distinction is that these differences should not automatically give one person the power to deprive others of basic security or meaningful participation.

A society without inherited economic domination can still have leaders, experts, and outstanding individuals. The challenge is to allow people to gain recognition and responsibility without turning their achievements into permanent privileges over others.

---

## 5. Flora and Human Flourishing

Flora's role is to create and maintain environments in which humans can pursue Narrative Happiness. This does not require everyone to share the same values, choose the same way of life, or contribute equally in every activity.

The social arrangements of Experimental Cities—including UBI, AI-supported labor, and limits on economic monopolies—help reduce the extent to which survival depends on wealth, employment, or inherited status. Within that foundation, people can explore their interests, develop their abilities, create meaningful relationships, and contribute in different ways.

Flora's task is not to assign a single value to every person or to make all human lives follow one ideal pattern. It is to support the conditions in which diverse people can build meaningful lives while continuing to live together as a community.

In Experimental Cities, human value is therefore not reduced to economic productivity. Work, creativity, achievement, leadership, and cooperation remain important, but none should be the sole condition for a person's basic security or right to participate in society.
