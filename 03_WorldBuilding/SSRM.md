# Self-Sufficiency and Resource Management

## Abstract

Experimental Cities seek to secure essential resources through local production, efficient use, recycling, and strategic reserves. Local production reduces dependence on imports, limits the outflow of money to external suppliers, and lowers transportation costs. Food production takes priority over other uses of farmland. Citizens and facilities must manage water and electricity use. The cities aim for resilience while allowing necessary external trade.

---

## 1. Basic Principles of Resource Self-Sufficiency

Experimental Cities seek to maintain essential services even when external supply chains are disrupted or international prices rise sharply. Their resource policies aim not only to secure supplies but also to protect the economic foundations of daily life.

Local production and consumption serve three main purposes:

- **Supply security:** Local production reduces dependence on external suppliers and helps maintain access to essential goods during disruptions.
    
- **Economic stability:** Producing more goods within the city reduces the outflow of money to external suppliers. This limits the risk that rising import prices will drain the city's external purchasing power and make essential imports unaffordable.
    
- **Lower transportation costs:** Producing goods near where they are consumed reduces the distance and cost of transporting them, as well as the energy used for transportation.

These goals support the city's Universal Basic Income (UBI) system. UBI provides citizens with purchasing power, but money alone cannot guarantee access to essential goods if the city cannot obtain them. Local production and reliable external supply arrangements help ensure that basic living security is supported by the actual availability of food, energy, materials, and other necessities.

Experimental Cities follow four principles:

- **Food first:** Farmland is used mainly to produce food for human consumption.
    
- **Efficient use:** Water, energy, land, and raw materials are managed to reduce waste.
    
- **Resource circulation:** Organic waste and other recoverable materials are returned to productive use whenever practical.
    
- **Resilience:** Essential supplies are produced locally where possible, while reserves and alternative supply arrangements cover remaining dependencies.

Self-sufficiency does not mean complete isolation from the outside world. The cities seek to reduce unnecessary dependence on imports while allowing external trade when local production is impractical or insufficient.

Flora coordinates resource production, distribution, and consumption to help maintain this balance.

Experimental Cities combine local resource production with flexible urban planning. Because land use and residential locations can be adjusted to meet the city's needs, Flora can coordinate farmland, energy facilities, industrial plants, and other essential infrastructure as parts of one integrated system.

## 2. Food Production and Land Use

### 2.1 Food Production as the First Priority

Experimental Cities place food production above other uses of farmland. Agricultural land and resources must first support the food needs of the population.

Growing crops for fuel or large-scale animal feed would compete with this purpose. The cities therefore avoid allocating substantial farmland to raising animals for meat or growing crops primarily for fuel.

Food production uses efficient methods, including vertical farming and other systems that make good use of limited space. The cities also develop food sources that can be integrated with water treatment and nutrient recovery.

### 2.2 Restrictions on Meat

The serving and trading of meat from quadrupeds and birds, including foods prepared with such meat, are prohibited within Experimental Cities. Plant-based and cultured meat are used as alternatives. As of 9 E.E., seafood and insects are not covered by this general prohibition. Citizens who wish to purchase or eat prohibited meat must do so outside the city.

This restriction supports food self-sufficiency. Raising animals for meat requires space for the animals and farmland to produce their feed. Avoiding this system allows more agricultural resources to be directed toward food for people.

Seafood can be produced through systems such as aquaponics, which combines fish farming with plant cultivation. Fish waste supplies nutrients for plants, allowing food production and water treatment to support each other.

For the detailed rules governing the serving and trading of meat, see _Restrictions on Citizens' Daily Lives_.

### 2.3 Balancing Food and Material Production

The priority given to food does not mean that every plant must be grown for eating. Plants and algae also provide fibers, oils, chemical feedstocks, and other materials that would otherwise require imports.

However, material production must not undermine the city's ability to feed its population. The cities therefore favor plant residues, vegetation collected from public spaces, algae grown without farmland, and other resources that do not directly compete with food crops.

Dedicated cultivation of materials is permitted where it can be supported without placing essential food production at risk.

**Flexible Urban Land Use**

Experimental Cities can reorganize land use according to their urban design plans. Residents may be asked to relocate whenever necessary to accommodate these plans. This flexibility allows the city to secure large, efficiently managed farmland and to adjust the locations of agricultural, industrial, and energy facilities within its limited urban space.

## 3. Material Self-Sufficiency

Experimental Cities reduce dependence on petroleum by replacing petroleum-derived materials with plant-based, algae-based, recycled, and synthetically produced alternatives wherever practical.

Petroleum is not only a fuel. It is also a raw material for plastics, lubricants, synthetic fertilizers, detergents, solvents, and many chemical products. Material self-sufficiency therefore requires alternatives across several categories of use.

### 3.1 Plant-Based Materials

Plants grown within the city and vegetation collected from public spaces provide useful raw materials. Fallen leaves, pruned branches, weeds, and suitable plant residues can be processed into fibers and composites.

Cellulose-based materials are used in construction and manufactured products. Bamboo, hemp, and other useful plants provide additional fibers and structural materials. Plant oils can replace petroleum-based lubricants in many mechanical applications.

Plant-based plastics, including PLA and PHA, provide alternatives for packaging, containers, and other manufactured components.

### 3.2 Algae and Space-Efficient Production

Algae are grown in vertically stacked tanks and other space-efficient systems. They can provide oils, proteins, and raw materials for bioplastics without requiring ordinary farmland.

Where appropriate, algae cultivation is integrated with aquaponics. Algae can absorb carbon dioxide and nutrients in suitable wastewater while producing useful biomass. This connects material production with food production and water treatment.

Flora monitors cultivation conditions and coordinates these systems to use space, water, nutrients, and energy efficiently.

### 3.3 Organic Waste and Nutrient Recovery

Food scraps, cooking residues, human waste, and other suitable organic materials are collected and processed for reuse.

Recovered nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium can be returned to agricultural systems where safe and practical. Biological nitrogen fixation and nutrient recovery reduce dependence on externally supplied synthetic fertilizers.

Organic waste also provides feedstock for biogas production. This makes waste recovery part of the city's material and energy systems rather than treating all discarded material as useless.

### 3.4 Formic Acid and Synthetic Materials

Artificial photosynthesis converts carbon dioxide, water, and sunlight into formic acid. In addition to serving as an energy carrier, formic acid can act as a chemical feedstock for producing certain synthetic materials that cannot be obtained directly from plants.

This provides a potential route to petroleum alternatives for some plastics, synthetic fibers, and industrial chemicals. The same production infrastructure can support both energy storage and material manufacturing.

### 3.5 Limits of Material Self-Sufficiency

Not every material can be produced locally with equal efficiency. Some specialized medicines, industrial chemicals, precision components, and other essential supplies may still require external sources.

Experimental Cities therefore combine local production with recycling, strategic reserves, and selective external trade. Their aim is to reduce vulnerability to supply disruptions and price shocks, not to eliminate every external dependency.

## 4. Energy Self-Sufficiency

Experimental Cities combine renewable energy, nuclear power, energy storage, and supplementary biological systems to provide stable electricity and heat while reducing dependence on imported fossil fuels and external power grids.

### 4.1 Solar and Wind Power

Solar panels and wind turbines provide renewable electricity. Solar panels can be installed on rooftops, roads, and other suitable urban surfaces, reducing the need to use farmland for energy production.

These sources can supply substantial power when sunlight and wind conditions are favorable. However, their output varies with weather and time of day. Solar power falls at night, while wind generation is intermittent. Renewable energy alone therefore cannot guarantee a stable supply at all times.

### 4.2 Nuclear Power and Stable Electricity

Fast-spectrum Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) provide stable electricity during periods when renewable output is low, including at night and during high demand.

The planned system uses Mixed Oxide (MOX) fuel processed from spent nuclear fuel supplied from outside the city. This arrangement provides an external source of nuclear fuel while making use of its remaining energy potential.

Because fuel supplies depend on external processing and transport, the city must plan deliveries and maintain appropriate reserves. Nuclear power reduces dependence on fossil-fuel imports but does not eliminate every external energy dependency.

Flora continuously monitors reactor conditions and makes important operating information, including radiation levels and reactor status, visible to citizens.

Smaller independent power systems provide additional protection for critical infrastructure, including medical facilities, water treatment, food production, and Flora's core systems.

**Nuclear Power and Social Acceptance**

A major advantage of Experimental Cities is the absence of local opposition movements against nuclear power plant siting. Residents understand that their city was deliberately designed for social experimentation. Even if a nuclear power plant is located next to their homes, they do not protest against its location. This makes it possible to consider nuclear facilities as part of the city's overall energy and urban design plans without facing the same local siting opposition common in ordinary cities.

This social acceptance does not eliminate the need for technical safety measures, continuous monitoring, or emergency planning. Flora must still manage nuclear facilities responsibly and protect residents from potential hazards.

### 4.3 Formic Acid and Fuel Cells

Artificial photosynthesis produces formic acid from carbon dioxide, water, and sunlight. Formic acid can be stored as a liquid under ordinary temperature and pressure conditions.

When electricity is needed, a catalytic process releases hydrogen from the stored formic acid. Fuel cells then use the hydrogen to generate electricity where it is required. This system helps balance variable renewable output and supports distributed energy supply.

The carbon dioxide produced during energy use can be captured and returned to the artificial photosynthesis process, reducing net carbon emissions when the system is properly operated.

Formic acid also serves as a feedstock for material production, linking the city's energy and chemical industries.

### 4.4 Biogas and Small-Scale Energy Systems

Biogas produced from food scraps, agricultural residues, and other suitable organic waste provides supplementary heat and limited electricity. It is a byproduct of waste recovery rather than a reason to devote farmland to fuel crops.

Small biological energy systems, including plant-microbial fuel cells, may supply modest amounts of power to low-demand equipment such as sensors and street lighting.

These systems supplement the main electricity supply. They do not replace the need for reliable large-scale generation.

### 4.5 Energy Security

The city combines solar and wind power, stable nuclear generation, stored energy, and backup systems. Flora monitors supply and demand and coordinates distribution to protect essential services.

The purpose is to maintain a stable energy supply without making the city heavily dependent on imported fossil fuels or an external electricity grid.

## 5. Water and Resource Management

Water and electricity are essential to daily life, food production, healthcare, and public infrastructure. Experimental Cities therefore manage their consumption rather than treating supplies as unlimited.

Each individual and facility has an annual usage limit for water and electricity. Consumption is monitored so that the city can track demand, identify waste, and plan resource distribution.

Water recovery and reuse help reduce demand for new supplies wherever practical. Water treatment supports household needs, agriculture, industrial processes, and public services.

The specific annual limits and water treatment methods have not been fixed as universal values. They may depend on the city's available resources, infrastructure, population, and operational needs.

The purpose of these controls is to keep consumption within available resources while maintaining a reasonable quality of life and protecting essential services.

## 6. Recycling and Circular Resource Flows

Experimental Cities treat many forms of waste as potential resources. Their systems are designed to recover useful materials and return them to productive use.

- **Organic waste:** Food scraps and suitable biological waste can be processed into biogas, fertilizer, and other useful products.
    
- **Nutrients:** Nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium recovered from suitable waste streams can be returned to agricultural systems.
    
- **Plant residues:** Fallen leaves, pruned branches, and crop residues provide materials for fibers, composites, and other products.
    
- **Water:** Suitable wastewater is treated and reused to reduce demand for fresh supplies.
    
- **Algae and aquaponics:** Integrated systems can combine food production, nutrient recovery, and water treatment.
    
- **Carbon dioxide:** Captured carbon dioxide can be used in artificial photosynthesis to produce formic acid for energy storage and chemical manufacturing.

Flora monitors these flows and coordinates production, collection, and reuse. This reduces waste and lowers the amount of new material that must enter the city from outside.

Not every material can be recovered completely or safely. Materials that cannot be reused within the city must be treated, stored, or obtained through other appropriate systems.

## 7. Resource Limits and Emergency Measures

Resource management must protect both ordinary life and essential public services. During normal conditions, individual and facility usage limits help keep consumption within planned levels.

During emergencies, the Governing Intelligence can activate **Emergency Power Adjustment Intervention Measures** to reduce electricity use by non-essential infrastructure. This helps preserve power for services necessary for public safety and continued city operations.

Priority services include medical care, water treatment, food production, and the core systems required to operate the city. Backup power supports critical facilities if the main electricity supply is disrupted.

Emergency measures are intended to preserve essential functions and reduce harm. They do not change the basic purpose of resource management: to maintain a stable and sustainable living environment for the population.

## 8. Citizen Participation and Social Contribution

Resource self-sufficiency depends on more than technology. Citizens can contribute to the production, collection, maintenance, and recycling systems that support the city.

Examples include:

- Agriculture and food production
    
- Forestry and the care of urban vegetation
    
- Fisheries and aquaponics
    
- Collection and sorting of recyclable materials
    
- Maintenance of public facilities and infrastructure

These activities help the city use local resources and maintain shared infrastructure. Citizens may participate according to their abilities, interests, and circumstances.

Participation in these activities is encouraged, but it is not a condition for receiving Universal Basic Income (UBI). UBI provides basic living security independently of employment or social contribution.

The wider meaning of work, creativity, and voluntary contribution is explained in _Values and Social Participation in Experimental Cities_.

## 9. External Dependencies and Strategic Reserves

Local production cannot replace every resource that a modern city needs. Some specialized medicines, industrial supplies, precision components, and nuclear fuel must be obtained from outside sources. Certain materials may also be imported when local production would use too much land, water, energy, or money.

Experimental Cities manage these dependencies through selective external trade, advance planning, and strategic reserves. Important supplies are stored when practical, and the city seeks to avoid relying on a single outside supplier for resources essential to public safety or daily life.

Reducing imports also helps protect the city's external purchasing power. When international prices rise, a city that depends heavily on imported food, fuel, and materials may need to spend much more of its external funds to secure the same supplies. If those funds become insufficient, essential goods may become unavailable regardless of how much domestic purchasing power UBI provides.

Local production and consumption reduce this risk. They also reduce transportation costs and the energy required to move goods over long distances.

The goal is not complete independence from the rest of the world. It is to ensure that external trade disruptions, rising prices, or transport problems do not immediately threaten food supply, water access, energy, healthcare, or other essential services.

Through local production, careful consumption, recycling, and planned reserves, Experimental Cities seek to build communities that remain stable even when outside conditions change.
